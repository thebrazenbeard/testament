## Why this map exists

The literary project cannot safely treat "the Merlin legend" as a single story. The source tradition is a network of manuscripts, poems, hagiography, chronicle, prophecy, and later synthesis whose pieces do not begin together.

This file tracks which literary layer may be used where.

## Layer A — Historical environment

### Arfderydd
- annalistic battle under 573 (possibly 575 depending chronology);
- oldest surviving Annales Cambriae witness records only `Bellum Armterid`;
- fuller participants and the "Merlin went mad" clause occur in later witnesses.

Use:
- battle as historical anchor;
- exact participants cautiously;
- Merlin's presence only as literary construction unless later source is being dramatized as later source.

### Rhydderch
Historically better anchored than Merlin through Adomnán's near-seventh-century Columba tradition.

Use:
- sixth-century northern ruler;
- no automatic direct relationship with the protagonist.

## Layer B — Early Welsh prophetic/wild-man corpus

The current Welsh Merlin Poetry Project treats seven poems as the major early corpus distributed through multiple medieval manuscripts.

Critical current control:
https://merlinpoetry.wales/
https://www.cardiff.ac.uk/news/view/2899063-discovering-the-true-origins-of-merlin

### Yr Afallennau
- first-person forest voice;
- speaker not explicitly named Myrddin in the Black Book witness;
- Black Book preserves excerpts, not the complete poem;
- combines personal/legend material with political prophecy;
- later witnesses must be consulted before declaring any detail absent.

Literary use:
apple tree as interlocutor, refuge, witness, and symbol of memory.
Do not make every transmitted stanza sixth-century speech.

### Yr Oianau
- first-person forest voice;
- speaker not explicitly named Myrddin in Black Book;
- addresses a pig;
- combines personal material with prophecy;
- preserved in several independent/related witnesses.

Literary use:
animal companionship; cold, sleeplessness, pursuit, social exile.
Keep political prophecies visibly later unless source criticism narrows them.

### Ymddiddan Myrddin a Thaliesin
- named speakers in manuscript apparatus;
- first portion concerns Maelgwn/Dyfed material;
- later portion concerns Arfderydd;
- Jarman placed it around late 11th century, but dating remains scholarly reconstruction.

Literary use:
not a historical meeting by default.
Best used as a later "meeting of authorities" in which tradition makes two prophetic voices speak.

### Cyfoesi Myrddin a Gwenddydd ei Chwaer
- current critical work treats poem as coherent rather than accretive;
- linguistic evidence currently points toward beginning of 13th century;
- relationship of Myrddin and Gwenddydd is central.

Literary use:
Book V or later memory layer, not sixth-century dialogue transcript.

### Gwasgargerdd Fyrddin yn y Bedd
- later textual tradition;
- project discovered ending absent from best-known major manuscripts.

Literary use:
posthumous voice / tradition speaking through the grave.

### Peirian Faban
- likely 12th century on current linguistic/historical indicators.

Literary use:
evidence for medieval prophetic persona, not historical Merlin.

### Y Bedwenni
- relevant early poem;
- current critical edition by David Callander (2026).

Literary use:
expand nature/prophecy network only after upstream analysis.

## Layer C — Lailoken / Kentigern complex

### Vita Merlini Silvestris
The surviving manuscript is late, though the narrative may draw on earlier Kentigern material.

The text names the wild man Lailoken and says that "some say" he was Merlin, explicitly leaving the identity uncertain.

Literary use:
this uncertainty is narratively valuable. A later Christian narrator can know of a Lailoken and know that others call him Merlin without being able to prove identity.

### Jocelin of Furness
Late 12th-century hagiography.

Literary use:
Christian reinterpretation layer; not sixth-century eyewitness.

## Layer D — Ambrosius

In Historia Brittonum the fatherless child and dragons belong to Ambrosius, not Merlin.

Literary rule:
tell Ambrosius independently before Geoffrey's fusion.

## Layer E — Geoffrey of Monmouth

### Historia Regum Britanniae / De gestis Britonum
Geoffrey substitutes Merlinus Ambrosius for the Ambrosius child-prophet and inserts the Prophecies.

### Vita Merlini
Geoffrey then supplies a radically different northern wild-man Merlin, drawing close to Arfderydd/Rhydderch/Celyddon material.

Current 2026 scholarly model:
multiple and potentially multidirectional traditions; two related figures first conflated by Geoffrey.

Literary use:
Geoffrey is the visible assembler, not a transparent transmitter.

## Layer F — Romance Merlin

Arthurian mentor, magician, shapeshifter, engineered conception, imprisonment, Nimue/Viviane, etc.

Literary use:
show the cultural afterlife of the constructed figure.

Never back-project into Layer A.

## Drafting principle

When the narrative is closest to the sixth century, the protagonist should know the least about being "Merlin."

As the centuries progress, the texts should know more about Merlin while knowing less about the original human being.
