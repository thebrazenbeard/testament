# Psychē — Life / Self / Soul V1

Status: LEXICAL_PACKET / REVIEW_REQUIRED

## Problem

English "soul" can smuggle a later body-versus-immortal-soul ontology into Greek passages where *psychē* may mean:
- life;
- living self;
- person;
- inner self;
- soul in an afterlife sense.

No one gloss works everywhere.

## Greek background

The Oxford Classical Dictionary notes that *psychē* develops substantially across Greek thought: from a "free-soul" associated with death/swoon in Homeric material toward a broader center of personhood/consciousness in later philosophy.

Reference:
- J. N. Bremmer, "psychē":
  https://academic.oup.com/edited-volume/61673/chapter-abstract/550494164

A. A. Long warns that even translating Platonic *psychē* mechanically as "soul" can mislead modern readers because the term can carry dimensions of agency, desire, accountability and personhood.

Reference:
- "Platonic Souls as Persons":
  https://academic.oup.com/book/44635/chapter-abstract/378654032

## Gospel high-risk sayings

Require passage-level analysis:
- losing/saving one's *psychē*;
- giving one's *psychē*;
- "what will it profit... gain world, lose psychē";
- anxiety about *psychē* and food;
- love God with whole psychē.

Possible renderings include:
- life;
- self;
- soul;
- being.

## Anti-dualism guard

A Gospel use of *psychē* does not automatically establish:
- Platonic substance dualism;
- an immortal detachable self in the later doctrinal sense;
- rejection of body.

Second Temple Jewish resurrection expectations often concern embodied restoration, so "soul" language must not overwrite that context.

## Testament consequence

Default to the rendering demanded by the scene.

Where "soul" would force ontology beyond the text, prefer "life" or "self" and expose the Greek term in the apparatus.
