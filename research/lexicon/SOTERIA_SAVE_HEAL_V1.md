# Sōzō / Sōtēria / Sōtēr — Save / Heal / Deliver / Saviour V1

Status: LEXICAL_PACKET / REVIEW_REQUIRED

## Problem

English "salvation" often immediately means:
"going to heaven after death."

The Greek word-family is much wider.

## Wider Greek environment

Theodora Suk Fong Jim's 2022 study describes *sōtēria* as a polyvalent concept involving:
- deliverance;
- preservation;
- safety;
- rescue;
- well-being;
at individual and communal levels.

Reference:
- "Soteria: A Polyvalent Concept":
  https://academic.oup.com/book/41429/chapter-abstract/352761128

Greek gods, rulers and benefactors could be called *sōtēr/sōteira* long before Christianity, often for this-worldly rescue.

Reference:
- *Saviour Gods and Soteria in Ancient Greece*:
  https://academic.oup.com/book/41429

## Gospel overlap: save / heal

The verb *sōzō* can occur in healing contexts where English translations alternate between:
- saved;
- healed;
- made well.

This matters in sayings such as "your *pistis* has *sōsei* you."

Translating "faith has saved you" may sound doctrinal where "trust has made you well" may better fit one scene—but neither choice should be global.

## Later Christological title

The New Testament also uses *Sōtēr* of God and Jesus in developed salvific/es­chatological contexts.

A 2022 *New Testament Studies* article notes multiple nuances across the title's occurrences.

Reference:
- "The Language of Imperial Cult and Roman Religion in the Latin New Testament: The Latin Renderings of 'Saviour'":
  https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/new-testament-studies/article/language-of-imperial-cult-and-roman-religion-in-the-latin-new-testament-the-latin-renderings-of-saviour/FAEBFB9A7A09AF48AC93F0F0E5B64011

## Testament consequence

Do not let "salvation" automatically mean postmortem destination.

Ask what danger, condition, illness, political situation, judgment or existential state the source imagines being saved from.
