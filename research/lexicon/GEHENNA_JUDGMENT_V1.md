# Gehenna / Judgment V1

Status: LEXICAL_PACKET / REVIEW_REQUIRED

## Problem

Many English Bibles translate *Gehenna* as "hell."

That imports a large later doctrinal package into a source term with its own Jewish history.

## Name/history

*Gehenna* derives from the Valley of Hinnom / Ge-Hinnom associated in biblical tradition with condemned cultic practices and judgment imagery.

By Hellenistic/Roman and later Jewish literature the term participates in eschatological punishment traditions whose exact duration, mechanism and geography vary.

## Synoptic variation

Chaim Milikowsky's study argues that Matthew and Luke's uses of Gehenna should be analyzed against varied early Jewish models of retribution rather than assuming one fixed doctrine of hell.

Reference:
- Chaim Milikowsky, "Which Gehenna? Retribution and Eschatology in the Synoptic Gospels and in Early Jewish Texts":
  https://doi.org/10.1017/S0028688500020038

## Translation rule

Where the Greek text says *Gehenna*, Testament should usually preserve **Gehenna** in the literary text or apparatus rather than silently choose:
- eternal hell;
- annihilation;
- purgatory;
- literal valley only.

The source context determines what can be said.

## Historical ceiling

Strong:
- Gehenna functioned as judgment language in early Jewish/Christian discourse.

Not established by the word alone:
- duration of punishment;
- conscious torment;
- postmortem geography;
- later medieval hell imagery.

## Testament consequence

Book III should not put Dante into Yeshua's mouth.

Book VIII may critique fear-based religion without pretending every Gehenna saying meant nothing more than a garbage dump; that popular reduction is itself historically weak.
