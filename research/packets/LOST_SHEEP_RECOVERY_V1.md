# Lost Sheep / Recovery V1

Status: SAYING_PACKET / REVIEW_REQUIRED

## Primary forms

- Matthew 18:12–14;
- Luke 15:3–7;
- Gospel of Thomas 107.

## Shared image, different literary jobs

All three forms preserve a shepherd, a large flock, one sheep that becomes separated, and special attention to recovering that one.

But the literary purposes differ.

Matthew places the parable inside community discourse about the "little ones" and concludes that the Father does not will that one of them be lost.

Luke places it in a sequence responding to complaints about Yeshua receiving "sinners" and interprets the recovery through repentance and heavenly joy.

Thomas 107 makes the lost sheep especially large/valuable and ends with the shepherd saying he loves it more than the ninety-nine.

These are not interchangeable endings.

## Source relationship

Under Q-based Synoptic models, Matthew/Luke may derive related forms from shared sayings tradition, but their differences raise questions about redaction and whether one evangelist has preserved a more compact form.

A social-scientific study by Ernest van Eck argues that Luke 15:4–6 preserves an early recoverable form and reads the parable against first-century pastoral/economic realities.

Reference:
- Ernest van Eck, "In the kingdom everybody has enough — A social-scientific and realistic reading of the parable of the lost sheep":
  DOI 10.4102/HTS.V67I3.1067

That is a reconstruction, not consensus by fiat.

## Thomas

Thomas 107 is important comparative evidence because it preserves the same basic narrative with a distinctive valuation motif.

Its historical weight depends on the still-disputed Thomas/Synoptic relationship.

John Halsey Wood argues broadly that Thomas often shows characteristics of dependence on canonical Gospel material.

Reference:
- "The New Testament Gospels and the Gospel of Thomas: A New Direction":
  DOI 10.1017/S0028688505000305

Other scholars defend greater independence for at least some Thomas sayings.

Therefore:
Thomas 107 is neither automatically independent nor automatically worthless.

## Historical ceiling

Strong:
- a lost-sheep/recovery parable belongs to early Jesus tradition.

Plausible:
- the historical parable concerned disproportionate attention to what had been lost.

Unresolved:
- original setting;
- whether repentance was explicit in the earliest form;
- whether "little ones" was original or Matthean framing;
- whether Thomas's "largest sheep" detail is early, secondary, or independent development.

## Testament consequence

Book III should not harmonize the endings.

The authored synthesis may draw:
"worth is not proportional to majority status."

But that formulation is ours, not the recovered wording of Yeshua.
