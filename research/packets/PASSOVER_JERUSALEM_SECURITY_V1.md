## Why Passover changes the scene

Passover brought:
- pilgrimage;
- intensified Temple activity;
- memory of liberation from foreign rule;
- large crowds;
- sacrificial logistics;
- Roman concern for public order.

Exact population estimates are notoriously difficult and should not be invented.

## Roman / elite security logic

Pilate normally governed from Caesarea but appears in Jerusalem in Passion tradition.

Roman provincial practice and the fortress/Temple geography make heightened festival security historically intelligible.

David Lloyd Dusenbury's 2023 reconstruction emphasizes the distinct jurisdictions and political actors—Pilate, Caiaphas, Antipas—around Yeshua's final days.

Reference:
- "Jurisdictions and Dramatis Personae":
  https://academic.oup.com/book/46147/chapter-abstract/404736798

A 2026 review of Andrade's *Killing the Messiah* summarizes its imperial-context thesis: local elites managing Roman order could react to Yeshua primarily as a destabilizing risk rather than because of abstract religious disagreement.

## Causal stack for Testament

A historically disciplined execution model should test interaction among:
- kingdom/royal symbolism;
- crowds;
- Temple disruption;
- prophetic judgment;
- priestly institutional risk;
- Roman anti-sedition/public-order logic;
- Passover timing.

No one layer needs to be the sole cause.

## Anti-blame rule

"Jewish leaders killed Jesus" is historically inadequate and ethically dangerous.

Roman authority performed crucifixion.

Local elite involvement may be historically plausible/significant, but:
- exact procedure is contested;
- Gospel blame allocation is literary/theological;
- later collective blame against Jews is not historical reconstruction.

## Manuscript consequence

The final week should feel like a collision of institutions under pressure.

Yeshua need not command an army to be perceived as risky during a politically sensitive pilgrimage festival with elevated public-order concern.
