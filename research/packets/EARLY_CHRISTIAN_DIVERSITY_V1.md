# Early Christian Diversity V1

Status: RESEARCH_PACKET / REVIEW_REQUIRED

## Core correction

Book V must not narrate the period after Yeshua as a single church gradually accumulating doctrine.

Questions of "which Christianity" and "whose Christianity" arise very early.

Reference:
- Karen L. King, "Which Early Christianity?", *Oxford Handbook of Early Christian Studies*:
  https://doi.org/10.1093/oxfordhb/9780199271566.003.0004

## First-generation diversity

Paul's own letters already preserve:
- conflict over circumcision;
- table fellowship;
- gentile identity;
- apostolic authority;
- competing missionaries;
- resurrection questions;
- charismatic practice.

This is not later heresy invading an originally uniform movement.

## Early second century

Ignatius's letters witness:
- developing episcopal authority;
- Eucharistic identity;
- conflict over docetic positions;
- Christian-Jewish boundary questions;
- martyrdom ideology.

Traditional early-second-century dating has been challenged in current scholarship; dating/authenticity discussions must remain visible.

Reference:
- Henry Chadwick, "Ignatius of Antioch":
  https://doi.org/10.1093/0199246955.003.0010
- Jonathon Lookadoo's dating review as discussed in *Past & Present*:
  https://academic.oup.com/past/article/261/1/3/6982747

## "Gnosticism" guard

"Gnosticism" is a contested umbrella category.

Oxford Classical Dictionary notes that the diversity of groups/material exposed especially by Nag Hammadi has led some scholars to resist treating "Gnosticism" as one coherent religion.

Reference:
- John Dillon, "Gnosticism":
  https://doi.org/10.1093/acrefore/9780199381135.013.2858

Testament should prefer text/group-specific names where possible:
- Valentinian;
- Sethian;
- Thomasine as a heuristic only;
- Basilidean;
- Marcionite;
- proto-orthodox;
- Jewish-Christian traditions.

## Canonical/noncanonical divide

The eventual canonical boundary should not structure the research before the sources are studied.

Francis Watson's *Connecting Gospels* deliberately examines relationships across the later canonical/noncanonical divide.

Reference:
- https://academic.oup.com/book/32601

## Manuscript consequence

Book V is not "heresies after Christianity."

It is a history of competing attempts to say what Yeshua meant, who had authority to say it, and which texts could stabilize those answers.
