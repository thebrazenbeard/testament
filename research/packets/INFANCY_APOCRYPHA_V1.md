## Why this lane matters

The canonical Gospels leave most of Yeshua's childhood undocumented.

Later Christians filled that silence with extensive narrative traditions.

These traditions are crucial reception evidence and poor default biography.

## Protevangelium of James

NASSCAL dates the composition to the latter half of the second century and describes it as a narrative focused substantially on Mary's birth, childhood and adolescence before extending into Yeshua's nativity.

Reference:
- https://www.nasscal.com/e-clavis-christian-apocrypha/protevangelium-of-james/

It profoundly influences later Marian tradition.

It is not a first-century childhood memoir.

## Infancy Gospel of Thomas

NASSCAL describes a text focused on Yeshua between roughly ages five and twelve, with episodes ranging from curses and dangerous childhood power to beneficent miracles and the Temple story.

Reference:
- https://www.nasscal.com/e-clavis-christian-apocrypha/infancy-gospel-of-thomas/

The work survives in a complex multilingual manuscript tradition.

Its narrative Yeshua cannot be silently combined with Luke's twelve-year-old Yeshua as though both were equal biographical memories.

## Later compilations

Arabic, Armenian, Syriac and Latin infancy traditions often reuse earlier material.

Examples:
- Armenian Infancy Gospel substantially depends on Protevangelium traditions while adding distinctive material.
- East Syriac History of the Virgin compiles canonical and apocryphal sources.
- Latin Nativity of Mary synthesizes earlier infancy traditions.

References:
- https://www.nasscal.com/e-clavis-christian-apocrypha/armenian-infancy-gospel/
- https://www.nasscal.com/e-clavis-christian-apocrypha/history-of-the-virgin-east-syriac/

## Manuscript consequence

Book I may use these texts to show how later communities imagined the missing years.

Book II must not import their childhood episodes as historical fact.

The missing years remain missing.
