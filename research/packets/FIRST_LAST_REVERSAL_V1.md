# First / Last Reversal V1

Status: SAYING_PACKET / REVIEW_REQUIRED

## Primary forms

- Mark 10:31;
- Matthew 19:30;
- Matthew 20:16;
- Luke 13:30;
- Gospel of Thomas 4.

## Transmission problem

The aphorism appears in multiple placements and slightly different formulations.

That instability is itself evidence that the saying circulated as a portable maxim that could be attached to different narrative/parabolic contexts.

In Mark 10:31 it follows the rich-man / discipleship sequence.

Matthew reproduces that placement in 19:30 and also uses an inverted version after the laborers-in-the-vineyard parable in 20:16.

Luke 13:30 uses the saying inside an eschatological banquet/judgment sequence.

Thomas 4 includes first/last language inside a saying about age, children and becoming one.

## Redaction and context

Andrew David Clarke's reading of Mark 10:17–31 emphasizes the first/last saying as a narrative corrective to disciples' self-congratulation and status assumptions.

Reference:
- Andrew David Clarke, "Do not Judge who is Worthy and Unworthy":
  DOI 10.1177/0142064X09104960

That illuminates Mark's literary use; it does not establish the original historical setting.

Broader sayings research stresses that Gospel authors contextualize inherited sayings to serve local narrative purposes.

Reference:
- Willem S. Vorster and J. Eugene Botha, "Redaction, Contextualization and the Sayings of Jesus":
  DOI 10.1163/9789004267381_020

## Thomas

Thomas 4 gives first/last language a different wisdom/reversal setting.

Its relation to the Synoptic saying is disputed.

Because Thomas's collection may contain both inherited and Gospel-related material, this logion requires saying-level analysis rather than blanket independence/dependence assumptions.

## Historical ceiling

Strong:
- first/last reversal is an early and multiply placed Jesus-associated aphorism.

Plausible:
- Yeshua used status-reversal language repeatedly or a memorable reversal saying circulated very early.

Unresolved:
- original narrative setting;
- exact wording/order;
- whether Matthew 20:16 is inherited with the parable or attached redactionally;
- whether Thomas 4 preserves an independent form.

## Ethical significance

The saying attacks stable rank.

But it should not be reduced to:
"poor people will simply swap places with rich people forever."

Different Gospel placements connect reversal to:
- wealth;
- discipleship;
- eschatology;
- labor/reward;
- status.

## Testament consequence

Book III should present the aphorism as a portable reversal saying with variant settings.

Book VIII may develop it into an anti-hierarchy principle, but the ancient source forms remain more varied than one slogan.
