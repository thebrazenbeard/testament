## Why meals matter

Ancient Mediterranean meals were not merely private calorie intake. They could stage status, belonging, hierarchy, reciprocity, purity, patronage, and exclusion.

The Oxford Handbook of the Synoptic Gospels treats meals as social and political scenes in which Gospel writers establish theological and communal agendas.

Reference:
- Soham Al-Suadi, "Food and Meals":
  https://doi.org/10.1093/oxfordhb/9780190887452.013.2

A broader Oxford treatment of early Christian communal meals emphasizes that dining arrangements could reproduce or challenge social status and group boundaries.

Reference:
- Dennis E. Smith, "Communal Meals":
  https://doi.org/10.1093/oxfordhb/9780198747871.013.12

## Historical question

A reputation for socially provocative table fellowship may be historically important, but each scene also carries Gospel-specific theological design.

Do not infer one universal "Jesus ate with everyone" policy from every later meal narrative.

## Purity guard

Meals with tax collectors or people labeled "sinners" should not automatically be described as violations of ritual-purity law. Cecilia Wassen argues that purity concerns play only a minor role in these meal traditions and that the conflict is better read primarily through social norms/boundaries unless a specific scene supplies purity evidence.

Reference:
- Cecilia Wassen, "Jesus' Table Fellowship with 'Toll Collectors and Sinners': Questioning the Alleged Purity Implications":
  DOI 10.1163/17455197-01402004

## Manuscript consequence

Book II's "Table" chapter should use meals to show social order physically: who reclines where, who serves, who is invited, who is watched, who becomes scandalous by being present.

The narrator should not reduce every meal to a modern inclusion slogan.
