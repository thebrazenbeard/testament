## Original-context problem

Modern Isaiah scholarship does not treat the "Servant Songs" as isolated Christian predictions waiting for one future individual.

The Oxford Handbook of Isaiah argues that the servant motif must be interpreted within Isaiah 40–54 and its diachronic development, with strong collective/social identification and later transformations into plural servants.

Reference:
- "The Servant(s) in Isaiah":
  https://academic.oup.com/edited-volume/38588/chapter-abstract/334630806

## Septuagint complication

The Old Greek translation can shift how the servant is heard.

A 2024 JTS article argues that OG Isaiah 41–45 individuates and messianizes servant language more strongly than the Masoretic form in those chapters.

Reference:
- Michael Wade Martin, "The Individuated, Messianic Servant of OG Isaiah 41–45":
  https://doi.org/10.1093/jts/flae002

This is extremely important for Testament:
Jewish Greek reception before Christianity can already transform scriptural figures.

Therefore later Christian individual/messianic reading is not simply "literal Hebrew meaning" versus invention.

## Isaiah 52:13–53:12

Christian tradition strongly identifies the suffering servant with Yeshua.

Historical scholarship disputes:
- whether pre-Christian Judaism already had a suffering-messianic reading;
- whether Yeshua self-applied servant imagery;
- whether the passion tradition developed the linkage after his death.

The debate itself is old and unresolved.

Reference:
- "The Suffering Servant between the Testaments":
  New Testament Studies 31 (1985), 481–497.

## NT reception

Isaiah is one of the most heavily used scriptural books in the New Testament.

Steve Moyise's Oxford survey notes its importance for:
- John the Baptist;
- Jesus's opposition;
- healing;
- poor/good-news traditions;
- Gentile inclusion;
- new exodus/new creation.

Reference:
- "Isaiah in the New Testament":
  https://doi.org/10.1093/oxfordhb/9780190669249.013.30

## Testament consequence

Book II may show followers interpreting Yeshua's suffering through Isaiah after the execution.

It must not narrate Isaiah as a first-person screenplay written about Jesus.

Book V should explicitly show how scriptural rereading helps create Christology.
