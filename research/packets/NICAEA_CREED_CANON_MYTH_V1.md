## Popular myth to block

"The Council of Nicaea chose the books of the Bible."

This is not an adequate historical description.

## What Nicaea primarily addressed

The Council of Nicaea (325 CE) is centrally associated with:
- the controversy over Arius;
- relation of Son to Father;
- creed/confession;
- ecclesial/canonical matters in the disciplinary sense;
- Paschal/Easter coordination.

Its principal historical importance is Christological and ecclesial, not a vote selecting the twenty-seven New Testament books.

## Canon formation was broader and longer

The latest Oxford Handbook of textual criticism treats Christian canon formation through:
- canon lists;
- manuscript evidence;
- citations;
- ancient statements;
over an extended historical process.

Reference:
- John D. Meade, "The Formation of the Christian Canon" (2025):
  https://academic.oup.com/edited-volume/61539/chapter-abstract/537116629

Gallagher and Meade's collection of early canon lists emphasizes that ancient lists vary and require careful interpretation.

Reference:
- *The Biblical Canon Lists from Early Christianity*:
  https://academic.oup.com/book/25501

Jens Schröter notes that categories such as accepted/disputed/rejected precede the widespread technical use of "canonical" and that canonical/noncanonical texts coexist across the second through fourth centuries.

Reference:
- "The Formation of the New Testament Canon and Early Christian Apocrypha":
  https://academic.oup.com/edited-volume/34876/chapter-abstract/298341286

## Nicaea reception

Later councils repeatedly appeal to "Nicaea" while disagreeing about what fidelity to Nicaea requires.

Reference:
- Mark S. Smith, *The Idea of Nicaea in the Early Church Councils, AD 431–451*:
  https://academic.oup.com/book/11236

That is useful evidence that even a famous council becomes a contested memory.
