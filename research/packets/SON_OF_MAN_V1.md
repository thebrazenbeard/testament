# Son of Man / Human One V1

Status: RESEARCH_PACKET / REVIEW_REQUIRED

## Why this phrase matters

The Greek expression usually translated "the Son of Man" appears more than eighty times in the four Gospels as speech attributed to Yeshua, yet it does not become a normal early-Christian title in the way "Christ," "Lord," or "Son of God" does.

That asymmetry is one reason scholarship speaks of the "Son of Man problem."

Reference:
- Benjamin E. Reynolds, "Son of Man," Oxford Bibliographies:
  https://doi.org/10.1093/obo/9780195393361-0231

## Major interpretive problems

The phrase may interact with several distinct backgrounds:
- ordinary Aramaic expressions using *bar (e)nash(a)*;
- Hebrew *ben adam* language;
- Daniel 7's "one like a son of man / human being";
- later Jewish apocalyptic interpretation, including the Parables of Enoch and 4 Ezra;
- Gospel-specific literary/theological development.

These cannot be collapsed into one lexical meaning.

## Daniel 7

Daniel 7 presents successive empires followed by divine judgment and the appearance of "one like a human being / son of man." The relationship between this figure and the "holy ones" has generated extensive Jewish and Christian interpretation.

Reference:
- "Book of Daniel," Oxford Classical Dictionary:
  https://academic.oup.com/edited-volume/61673/chapter-abstract/548080178

## Saying clusters

Testament should classify Son of Man sayings at least into:
1. present-earthly authority/activity;
2. suffering/death sayings;
3. future/apocalyptic coming/judgment sayings.

The historical question must be asked separately for each cluster.

A saying's presence in a Gospel does not prove Yeshua coined its final Greek wording.

## Critical negative control

Why do early Christian authors rarely address Yeshua directly as "the Son of Man" outside Gospel speech?

That oddity can support arguments for preserved Yeshua tradition, but it does not by itself establish the authenticity of every Son of Man saying.

## Manuscript consequence

Book III should preserve the phrase's strangeness rather than translating it into one settled doctrine.

Book II should not make Yeshua explain the phrase to readers using later Christology.

Book VII may compare the phrase with agent/representative models only as modern analogy, never lexical translation.
