## Core rule

The Dead Sea Scrolls are context, comparison, and textual evidence.

They are not a hidden Christian archive.

## What the corpus gives Testament

The Scrolls illuminate Jewish diversity in:
- scripture and textual plurality;
- law and halakhah;
- purity;
- community discipline;
- calendars;
- priesthood;
- covenant;
- wisdom;
- angelology;
- cosmic conflict;
- resurrection/judgment;
- messianic expectation;
- scriptural interpretation;
- apocalyptic identity.

Oxford's handbook emphasizes that major disputes remain about Qumran archaeology, sect identity, and the relationship of the corpus to wider Judaism.

Reference:
- *The Oxford Handbook of the Dead Sea Scrolls*:
  https://academic.oup.com/edited-volume/34366

## Messiah plurality

Do not assume Qumran had one "the Messiah."

Different texts preserve different expectations:
- priestly figures;
- royal/Davidic figures;
- prophetic/es­chatological figures;
- paired messianic expectations;
- texts where no singular messianic agent is central.

The Damascus Document alone shows how law, leadership and messianism can be intertwined over a long textual history.

Reference:
- Charlotte Hempel, *The Damascus Document*:
  https://academic.oup.com/book/39774

Permitted comparisons include:
- kingdom/judgment;

## Witness value

The Scrolls also matter because they preserve Hebrew/Aramaic biblical and nonbiblical manuscripts from the Second Temple period, constraining what textual forms and concepts actually existed before/around Yeshua.

## Testament consequence

Book I should make Second Temple Judaism look plural enough that Yeshua is one Jewish argument among many, not Judaism's external critic.

Book III should use Scroll parallels as context unless direct dependence is separately established.
