# Ask / Seek / Knock V1

Status: SAYING_PACKET / WITNESS_CONTROL_ACTIVE / REVIEW_REQUIRED

## Primary forms

- Matthew 7:7–11;
- Luke 11:9–13;
- Thomas 2 and 92 as related seeking traditions rather than exact parallels.

## Synoptic form

Matthew and Luke share:
- ask / receive;
- seek / find;
- knock / opened;
- parent-child gift analogy.

They diverge in the climax:
- Matthew: the Father gives "good things";
- Luke: the Father gives the Holy Spirit.

That difference is theologically meaningful and should not be harmonized away.

## Literary dependence

Under Q models, the shared Matthew/Luke core belongs to double tradition.

Under direct-dependence models, the relation is explained differently.

Either way, Matthew + Luke is not automatically two independent historical witnesses.

## Thomas

Thomas's seeking sayings use a different revelatory framework:
seeking leads to finding, disturbance, wonder/rule.

These are useful comparative sayings but not enough to establish one common original without a transmission argument.

### Thomas 2 has Greek and Coptic witnesses

P.Oxy. IV 654 (British Library Papyrus 1531) is a Greek papyrus leaf dated approximately 200–250 CE. It preserves the opening of Gospel of Thomas through logion 7, so Thomas 2 is genuinely part of the surviving Greek Thomas evidence.

That matters, but the papyrus is fragmentary. The missing parts of lines are restored by editors; restoration does not become direct papyrus readback merely because it is persuasive.

The complete Coptic Thomas 2 in Nag Hammadi Codex II preserves the same broad seek/find trajectory but is not textually identical to the published Greek reconstruction. The Greek form is commonly reconstructed with a progression through finding, astonishment, reigning and rest; the Coptic form proceeds through finding, disturbance/astonishment and reigning without the same final rest clause.

Testament therefore records an actual intra-Thomas transmission difference rather than silently choosing one form.

### Thomas 92 is a different witness problem

Thomas 92 is preserved in Nag Hammadi Codex II in Coptic.

It is not preserved in P.Oxy. 654:
that papyrus stops within logion 7.

Nor is Thomas 92 preserved in the other surviving Greek Thomas fragments currently registered by the project.

Therefore:
- Thomas 2 = direct Greek + Coptic witness coverage;
- Thomas 92 = direct Coptic witness coverage only in the currently bound evidence.

A Greek Thomas 92 may be reconstructed for scholarly comparison, but it is not an extant Greek manuscript reading.

### Relationship to Matthew and Luke

Matthew 7:7–11 and Luke 11:9–13 preserve the tight ask / seek / knock triad plus a parent-child gift analogy.

Their climaxes differ:
- Matthew: good things;
- Luke: Holy Spirit.

Thomas 2 and 92 are related seeking traditions, not exact alternate manuscripts of the Synoptic unit.

The overlap is enough to justify comparison.
It is not enough to prove:
- direct Thomas dependence on Matthew or Luke;
- Synoptic dependence on Thomas;
- Thomasine independence;
- one original mega-saying containing all of the forms.

The project therefore keeps two separate questions visible:
1. Matthew/Luke literary relation;
2. Thomas/Synoptic relation.

Neither can be solved merely by counting similar words.

## Historical ceiling

Strong:
- early Jesus tradition links persistent seeking/prayer with divine response.

Unresolved:
- exact original sequence;
- Matthew's "good things" versus Luke's "Holy Spirit";
- Thomas's relation.

## Testament consequence

The final book should not weaponize this saying into:
"ask hard enough and reality must give you what you want."

Ancient prayer confidence is not a guarantee of outcome control.
