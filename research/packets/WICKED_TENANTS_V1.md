# Wicked Tenants / Vineyard V1

Status: SAYING_PACKET / WITNESS_CONTROL_ACTIVE / REVIEW_REQUIRED

## Primary forms

- Mark 12:1–12;
- Matthew 21:33–46;
- Luke 20:9–19;
- Thomas 65–66.

## Scriptural environment

The vineyard image strongly evokes Isaiah 5 and broader Israel/vineyard tradition.

The Synoptic versions are embedded in Jerusalem/Temple conflict and increasingly invite allegorical interpretation:
- owner;
- tenants;
- servants;
- beloved son;
- destruction/replacement;
- rejected stone.

## Historicity difficulty

The parable can be read as:
- Yeshua's prophetic critique;
- an allegory of salvation history;
- post-Easter Christian self-understanding;
- anti-leadership rather than anti-Israel polemic;
- later supersessionist development.

Matthew's expansion is especially dangerous if read as an unmediated transcript of Yeshua because later Christian anti-Jewish replacement theology can be imported backward.

## Recent Temple scholarship

Michael Barber's 2023 study treats the Wicked Tenants among traditions relevant to Temple/priestly imagery in historical-Jesus research while explicitly foregrounding modern methodological concerns about authenticity and anti-Temple bias.

## Thomas

Thomas 65 preserves a tenants story and Thomas 66 a rejected-stone saying in separate adjacent logia.

This is important comparative data.

It does not automatically prove that the Synoptic combination is late or that Thomas preserves pre-Synoptic independence.

Thomas 65 and 66 are directly extant in the Coptic Gospel of Thomas in Nag Hammadi Codex II.

No surviving Greek Thomas fragment currently bound by Testament reaches either saying.

Therefore this cluster is Coptic-only on the Thomas side.

### One work, two adjacent logia

Thomas 65 preserves the vineyard/tenant narrative.

Thomas 66 immediately follows with the rejected-stone saying as a separate logion.

That adjacency is important literary evidence.

It does not by itself prove:
- that the two sayings circulated separately before Mark;
- that Mark was the first to combine them;
- that Thomas is independent;
- that Thomas's arrangement is chronologically earlier.

The safe claim is narrower:

**the extant Thomas form places related tenants and rejected-stone material in two adjacent sayings rather than one joined narrative-scriptural complex.**

Published Thomas translations consistently preserve this adjacent 65/66 arrangement.

### Synoptic contrast

Mark 12 joins:
- vineyard/tenants;
- servants;
- beloved son/heir;
- destruction;
- rejected-stone scripture;
- Jerusalem authority conflict.

Matthew and Luke transmit related complexes under probable Markan dependence.

Thomas preserves much of the tenants narrative but separates the rejected-stone saying into the next logion.

That difference is useful for redaction history.
It is not a chronology machine.

### Anti-supersession guard

This is a high-risk passage for historical overreach.

Matthew's form in particular can be recruited into later replacement theology.

Book III must not convert:
- narrative judgment;
- leadership conflict;
- vineyard imagery;
- son/heir imagery;
into the historical claim:

> God rejected the Jews and transferred the kingdom to Christians.

That is later theological interpretation, not an evidence-floor reconstruction.

### Current historical ceiling

Established:
- the Synoptic tradition preserves a tenants/vineyard complex;
- Thomas 65 and 66 preserve adjacent related material in NHC II;
- Thomas's rejected-stone saying is separate from the tenants logion in the extant Coptic form.

Not established:
- Thomasine independence;
- historical originality of the separated form;
- exact degree of allegorization before Mark;
- original identity of the tenants/servants/son;
- supersessionist meaning.

## Historical ceiling

Plausible:
- a vineyard/tenant conflict parable may derive from Yeshua.

High confidence:
- Synoptic authors/redactors use it within Jerusalem authority conflict.

Unresolved:
- degree of allegorization before Mark;
- original referents;
- Thomas dependence;
- whether "son" had messianic/Christological force in the earliest form.

## Testament consequence

Book III must avoid turning the parable into "God took the kingdom from the Jews and gave it to Christians."

That later supersessionist reading is not allowed to sneak into the historical voice.
