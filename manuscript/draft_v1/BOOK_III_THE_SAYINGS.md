# Book III — The Sayings

## A Note Before the Sayings

These are not stenographic transcripts.

They are an authored literary arrangement of teachings preserved across early Jesus traditions. Some sayings have strong Synoptic embedding. Some appear in multiple forms. Some are textually unstable. Some have parallels in Thomas or the Didache whose dependence remains disputed. Some may be later community speech placed into Yeshua's mouth.

Where this draft creates a coherent voice, that coherence belongs partly to the author.

The purpose is not to manufacture the lost Aramaic.

It is to let the remembered questions stand together.

## On the Kingdom

Do not point too quickly and say, There.

The reign of God was remembered as coming and as already near, as seed and harvest, as banquet and judgment, as something hidden and something that overturns visible rank.

One tradition says it cannot be mapped by pointing here or there. The Greek phrase that follows can be heard as within you, among you, or in your midst, and the argument has lasted because the words permit more than one serious reading.

Thomas remembers related kingdom language more explicitly inward and outward, but Thomas does not get to decide Luke's Greek retroactively.

So keep the tension.

The kingdom may be near without being private.
It may be among you without being owned by an institution.
It may change persons without reducing itself to psychology.
It may threaten rulers without becoming another empire.

The author will later reinterpret kingdom as governance of the self and the world by repeated local choice.

Yeshua did not speak in control-system jargon.

## On God

Do not make piety an excuse for cruelty.

That is not a quotation. It is the line that forms when mercy, neighbor-love, prayer, prophetic criticism, and the remembered priority of persons over displays of righteousness are placed beside each other.

The traditions call God Father with unusual intimacy in some places, but "Father" is not a license to turn the divine into a larger version of an abusive household ruler.

The ancient word carries its own social world.

The final ethic is ours.

## On Neighbor and Enemy

Love of neighbor was already Torah.

The remembered radicalization is the refusal to let hostility define the boundary of moral concern.

Matthew and Luke preserve enemy-love and non-retaliation material in different arrangements. Didache carries a related cluster, but its literary relation to the Gospels remains disputed. A later expanded form of Matthew even absorbs language resembling Luke, which is a useful warning: transmission can erase difference.

The saying survives the uncertainty.

Do not become a copy of the person who harms you.

That is my paraphrase, not ancient wording.

Turn the other cheek can be made into a weapon if preached to the powerless by people who will not restrain the violent.

Enemy love cannot mean that.

It can mean refusing to let hatred become the government inside you.

It can mean breaking a retaliatory chain while still stopping harm.

That second sentence belongs to Book VIII's authored ethic.

## On Wealth and Debt

Debt in the traditions is both economic and moral because ancient life allowed the two fields to touch without making them identical.

Matthew's prayer remembers debts.
Luke remembers sins and people indebted to us.
Parables remember creditors, servants, landlords, workers, estates, coins, rents, and impossible obligations.

Do not spiritualize the economy until the poor disappear.

A debtor needs release in more than one sense.

A hungry person does not need an elegant metaphor for bread.

The kingdom tradition repeatedly turns status upside down, but poverty is not automatically virtue and wealth is not automatically a complete biography of vice.

The sharper question is what possession does to obligation.

What do you owe because you have enough to refuse not to see the person who does not?

## On Power and Status

The first want to remain first.

That is why reversal sayings endure.

Children, servants, the least, the last, the excluded, the person outside the honor system—these figures become pressure against rank.

A movement that turns reversal into a new hierarchy has heard the sentence and protected itself from the meaning.

## On Purity and Hypocrisy

Purity cannot be treated as one foolish ancient obsession from which modern people have escaped.

Purity structured real Jewish religious worlds and had many forms.

The remembered Yeshua sometimes relocates the moral crisis from contamination outside the person toward intention, speech, desire, mercy, and conduct.

That move is powerful.

It should not be exaggerated into a claim that Judaism cared only about externals or that Yeshua abolished concern for embodied practice.

Hypocrisy is simpler.

The mask becomes the self when it is worn long enough.

## On Prayer

The prayer tradition is not one immutable line of text.

Its forms differ.

Even Luke's kingdom petition has a remarkable variant family in which some witnesses preserve a petition for the Holy Spirit to come and cleanse.

That does not prove an original form merely because it is striking.

It does prove that prayer lived in transmission.

Pray, then, without pretending the surviving wording fell untouched from the sky.

Ask for daily need.
Ask for release.
Release what is owed to you where release is just.
Ask not to become what temptation wants.

Then stand up and participate in the answer.

The final sentence is authored.

## On Judgment

Judgment is easy when you are the judge.

The traditions repeatedly turn judgment back upon the observer.

Measure becomes reciprocal.
The speck and log become comic violence against self-deception.
Condemnation becomes dangerous when it conceals the same structure in the condemner.

This is not moral relativism.

A person can be wrong.

The warning is epistemic before it is permissive: your certainty about another person does not exempt your own vision from inspection.

## On Family

Yeshua tradition can sound brutal toward family.

Leaving.
Dividing.
Refusing ordinary kinship priority.
Calling disciples into a new household.
Thomas preserves harsh renunciation forms too.

The evidence does not support one easy slogan.

Ancient discipleship may genuinely have imposed social costs that modern inspirational reading domesticates.

Do not turn those costs into a universal command to abandon people.

Do not erase them either.

Movements that reorder loyalty create new families and new injuries.

Both deserve to remain visible.

## On Seeing

People see what they have categories to see.

A sign can become miracle, threat, deception, coincidence, fulfillment, or evidence depending on the observer.

That does not mean all interpretations are equal.

It means perception is never the whole argument.

The traditions love sight metaphors because misunderstanding can coexist with eyesight.

You can look directly at a person and see only the role you need them to occupy.

## Parables

A parable is not an encrypted doctrine with one password.

Seed.
Soil.
Debt.
Workers.
Tenants.
Sheep.
Banquet.
Treasure.
Household.
Judge.
Father.
Son.
Neighbor.

Their economies and social arrangements are not documentary photographs simply because they sound concrete.

Their force often lies in rearrangement.

The expected person fails.
The wrong person acts.
The small thing becomes large.
The valuable thing is missed.
The excluded person sees.
The owner returns.
The debt cannot be paid.
The lost thing becomes worth searching for.

A parable is a machine for moving the listener one step and then revealing that the floor moved too.

That is my description.

The stories are older.

## Hard Sayings

Not everything softens.

There are sayings about judgment, division, abandonment, fire, exclusion, hatred of kin, wealth, and impossible demand.

A modern ethical project should not erase them to make Yeshua safe.

Some may be redaction.
Some may be authentic.
Some may be hyperbole.
Some may be apocalyptic.
Some may preserve the cost of itinerant commitment.
Some may be irreducibly difficult.

A teacher worth studying need not be a teacher we can domesticate.

## Sayings That May Not Be His

There is no honest Gospel of Certainty hiding beneath the Gospels.

Communities remembered, arranged, interpreted, translated, defended, harmonized, and sometimes expanded.

The right question is not simply, Did Jesus say this?

Ask:

How early is the form?
How many sources actually stand behind it?
Are those sources dependent?
Does the wording fit the author's redactional habits?
Is the manuscript stable?
What Jewish context makes the saying intelligible?
What later theology might have shaped it?
What part remains even after uncertainty is admitted?

Then make the hardest distinction.

A saying can be profound without being historical.

A saying can be historical without being profound.

Truth is not protected by assigning every good sentence to a famous mouth.
