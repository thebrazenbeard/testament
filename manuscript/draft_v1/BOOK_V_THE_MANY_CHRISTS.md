# Book V — The Many Christs

## 1. Before Christianity Became Singular

There was no Nicene Christianity on the morning after Yeshua's death.

There was no twenty-seven-book New Testament.
No universal creed.
No global bishopric.
No settled doctrine of two natures.
No agreed relation between Israel's God, Yeshua, Spirit, Torah, gentiles, empire, resurrection, and the age to come.

There were people.

They remembered.
Argued.
Prayed.
Interpreted.
Competed.
Shared meals.
Refused meals.
Claimed visions.
Claimed authority.
Wrote letters.
Copied stories.
Corrected opponents.
Buried teachers.
Named successors.

The history of Christianity begins in plurality before it becomes a story about unity.

## 2. James's Yeshua

James stands close to the problem because family is hard to theologize away completely.

Paul identifies him as the brother of the Lord and treats him as major Jerusalem authority.

That does not tell us James's full theology.

It tells us that a later picture in which the family is irrelevant cannot simply be projected backward.

James represents one gravitational center: Jerusalem, Jewish continuity, kinship, authority, and a movement that had not ceased to be entangled with Israel.

Later Jewish-Christian groups would be associated with many claims, but those later reports cannot all be poured backward into James.

The temptation to recruit him as the pure alternative to Paul is as distorting as the temptation to erase him.

## 3. Peter's Yeshua

Cephas exists before the papacy.

That sentence should be obvious and often is not.

Paul knows him as a person and opponent.
Early tradition remembers him as an appearance recipient.
Gospels make him confessor, failure, rock, shepherd, denier, restored disciple.

Later institutions enlarge him further.

The historical Peter is neither reducible to later Roman claims nor separable from the growth of those claims.

Memory accumulates around useful figures.

Peter became useful to almost everyone.

## 4. Paul's Christ

Paul writes earlier than the narrative Gospels and sounds, to modern ears, strangely uninterested in biography.

That is because he is answering other questions.

Who belongs?
What has the cross accomplished?
What does resurrection mean?
What happens to gentiles?
What is faithfulness?
What role does Torah play?
What body will be raised?
How should assemblies live while awaiting transformation?

Paul's Christ is cosmic without ceasing to be crucified.

His letters also reveal how contested his authority was.

The man who later became scripture spent part of his career defending whether he counted as an apostle.

Canon has a sense of humor.

## 5. Mark's Son

Mark's Yeshua moves fast.

People misunderstand.
Disciples fail.
Demons recognize.
Crowds press.
The road turns toward suffering.

The earliest recoverable ending of Mark leaves women in fear and silence after the tomb announcement.

Later transmitters did not always tolerate that ending.

The additions themselves are evidence—not of what Mark originally wrote, but of what readers felt a Gospel should provide.

## 6. Matthew's Teacher

Matthew remembers Yeshua through scripture and Torah.

Fulfillment language saturates the narrative.
Teaching becomes architecturally important.
Community discipline becomes explicit.
Peter's symbolic role expands.
The mountain echoes earlier scriptural worlds.

Matthew is not simply Mark plus extra facts.

He is an interpreter.

That does not make him dishonest.

It makes him an author.

## 7. Luke's Lord

Luke organizes history.

Israel.
Yeshua.
Spirit.
Church.
Mission.
Jerusalem and the nations.

The poor, outsiders, women, meals, prayer, reversal, and Spirit receive distinctive emphasis. Acts continues the story and often smooths conflict that Paul's own letters leave jagged.

Luke's achievement is enormous.

So is the need to read him as Luke.

## 8. John's Logos

John begins before Bethlehem.

Not with a census.
Not with a manger.
With Logos.

The Fourth Gospel gives us the most explicit sustained identity discourse among the canonical Gospels. Its Yeshua speaks long theological speeches unlike the Synoptic teaching form.

That does not make John useless for history.

It makes unit-level judgment necessary.

A vivid place-name may preserve local memory while a long discourse reflects Johannine theology. Both can be true.

"Jewish or Greek" is too small a binary for John's conceptual world.

Scripture, Wisdom, Logos, Spirit, Temple, festival, revelation, and community conflict belong together.

John's Jesus becomes one of the most consequential interpretations of Yeshua ever produced.

## 9. Thomas's Living Jesus

Thomas strips away plot.

No crucifixion narrative.
No empty tomb story.
A voice of sayings.

Some sayings resemble Synoptic forms. Some differ radically. Greek fragments preserve parts of the work. A later Coptic codex preserves the complete collection. Dependence and dating remain disputed saying by saying.

Thomas is neither a magical independent transcript nor disposable late fantasy.

Its value changes by unit.

In one place, Greek Thomas preserves direct contact language close enough to Luke to make dependence arguments serious.
In another, only Coptic survives.
In another, the saying develops into a distinct interiorizing world.

Thomas shows what happens when Yeshua becomes a living interpreter whose words are puzzles for transformation.

## 10. Jewish-Christian Gospels

Some Gospels survive because opponents quoted them.

This is a brutal form of preservation.

Texts associated with Hebrews, Nazarenes, Ebionites, and other Jewish-Christian trajectories are fragmentary and mediated. Their names and relationships are not always stable.

We should not reconstruct whole books out of hostile fragments and wishful confidence.

But their existence matters.

The stream was broader than the library that survived.

## 11. Marcion

Marcion is easier to condemn than to understand.

That is usually a warning.

He confronted one of Christianity's deepest problems: how can the God revealed in Christ be reconciled with the creator and scriptures as he understood them?

His answer was radical separation.

He used a Gospel related to Luke and a Pauline collection. Later opponents preserve much of what we know, which means reconstruction must account for hostile mediation.

He did not simply wake up and delete verses from our finished New Testament.

The textual history is harder than that.

Marcion demonstrates that "Christian" did not once mean one agreed answer about scripture, God, creation, or continuity with Israel.

## 12. Valentinian Worlds

Valentinian traditions asked different questions.

Why is reality fractured?
Why are persons ignorant of what they are?
What does revelation repair?
What kind of Father stands beyond the world as ordinarily known?
What is salvation if ignorance itself is bondage?

Later polemic compresses these traditions into slogans.

The surviving texts complicate them again.

Emanation.
Fullness.
Error.
Knowledge.
Savior.
Resurrection.
Sacrament.
Identity.

Not one system.
Not one "Gnosticism."
A family of serious Christian metaphysical experiments.

## 13. The Church That Won

"Won" is too simple if it implies conspiracy.
It is still useful if it means survival, institutional reach, textual reproduction, theological consolidation, imperial entanglement, and boundary enforcement.

Irenaeus argued for a fourfold Gospel.
Bishops became increasingly important.
Rules of faith stabilized reading.
Texts acquired authority through use, argument, copying, and exclusion.
Creeds came later.
Councils came later still.

The eventual canon is a historical achievement.

It is not proof that every rejected text was earlier.
It is not proof that every accepted text was historically transparent.
It is not proof that history naturally wanted twenty-seven books.

The Many Christs are not many historical Yeshuas.

They are many answers generated by one historical gravitational center.

That may be the most important fact about the second century.

Yeshua did not become one thing after death.

He became a field in which communities argued about God, humanity, Israel, body, spirit, creation, law, empire, death, and hope.

The argument was Christianity before Christianity became an answer.
