# Book IV — Death and What Followed

## 1. After

Death is a kind of editing.

It removes the person and leaves everyone else fighting over the sentence.

The surviving traditions do not give us a camera in the hours after the crucifixion. They give us grief after it has already become proclamation, memory after it has already become community, and stories preserved by people who knew what the ending was supposed to mean.

So begin smaller.

He was dead.

Whatever his followers thought the kingdom meant before Jerusalem, Rome had supplied an answer made of wood, iron, blood, and public humiliation.

Movements do not survive automatically after that.

Leaders die.
Followers scatter.
Fear is rational.

The fact that this movement did survive requires explanation.

It does not yet require one explanation.

## 2. The Body

Burial traditions appear early enough to matter and differently enough to resist laziness.

Joseph of Arimathea enters the Gospel accounts in varying detail. Tomb traditions grow more developed as the narratives move. Archaeology and legal custom can constrain what was possible; they cannot produce the missing chain of custody.

A Roman execution victim could be buried.
A crucified body could also be denied ordinary dignity.
Passover, local practice, patronage, and authority all matter.

The responsible claim is not "we know there was no tomb."
It is not "the empty tomb is proven."

It is that burial belongs inside the early tradition while its exact form remains disputed.

Historical confidence becomes weaker as the proposition becomes more ornate.

## 3. Mary

Women are difficult to remove from the resurrection memory.

That does not mean every sequence is recoverable.

Mary Magdalene becomes especially important. The Gospels position women differently, and later Christian authority structures did not always preserve the implications of female witness with equal enthusiasm.

The literary temptation is obvious: write one perfect dawn.

I will not.

Let there be more than one memory.

A woman goes.
A woman sees absence.
A woman hears.
A woman reports.
Others do not believe.
Another tradition begins elsewhere.
One Gospel ends in fear at the earliest recoverable form.
Another supplies encounters.

The contradiction is not noise around a single clean transcript.

It is part of the evidence.

## 4. Peter

Paul's early appearance list gives Cephas a privileged place in one remembered sequence.

The Gospels turn Peter into a larger drama of confession, failure, denial, restoration, commission, and later authority.

Those are not one proposition.

The historical Cephas knew Paul.
Paul fought with him.
Later communities claimed him.

The Petrine figure became a foundation strong enough for institutions to build on.

The man underneath the foundation remains less accessible.

## 5. James

James is harder for later imagination to ignore once Paul is allowed to speak first.

Paul calls him the Lord's brother.
Paul treats him as a major Jerusalem figure.
An early appearance tradition names him.

That combination matters.

Whatever the family tension in Gospel memory, the brother of Yeshua becomes central after Yeshua's death.

A movement whose leader dies and whose brother later holds authority has more human continuity than theological retellings sometimes admit.

The family did not disappear into doctrine.

## 6. They Said He Lived

This is historically strong:

Very early followers proclaimed resurrection.

They did not merely preserve his sayings as the words of a dead teacher.

They said God had acted.

Paul writes early enough that resurrection proclamation is not a late fourth-century invention. He knows named leaders. He knows appearance traditions. He knows controversy about the nature of resurrection because communities were already arguing about what their own central claim meant.

That still leaves the largest question open.

What happened?

## 7. What Did They See?

The sources use more than one kind of language.

Appearance.
Vision.
Touch.
Meal.
Recognition.
Non-recognition.
Heavenly revelation.
Empty space.
Commission.
Fear.

Different traditions emphasize embodiment differently.

Several explanatory models remain live at the level of historical method:

bodily resurrection;
visionary or revelatory experience;
grief-related experiences;
socially transmitted interpretation;
legendary development;
mixed models;
unknown cause.

A theological reader may choose one.
A historian must justify every step.

The existence of alternatives does not disprove resurrection.
The sincerity of proclamation does not prove the mechanism.

This is the line the book must hold.

## 8. Jerusalem

The earliest movement remains connected to Jerusalem.

Paul's letters recover some of that world indirectly and sometimes more sharply than Acts because Paul is writing inside conflict rather than narrating a unified origin story after the fact.

James.
Cephas.
Other apostles.
Questions about circumcision.
Table fellowship.
Gentiles.
Authority.
Collection for the poor.
Who gets to define belonging.

Acts gives a powerful theological history.

Paul gives evidence that harmony was expensive.

Both matter.
They do not always agree.

## 9. Saul

Paul did not meet Yeshua during the public ministry, as far as our surviving sources establish.

He nevertheless became one of the most consequential interpreters of Yeshua in history.

His own letters describe a revelatory encounter and a radical redirection of allegiance. Acts narrates the experience in multiple forms.

The differences matter.

The common center matters too: a former opponent becomes a missionary who believes the crucified Yeshua has been revealed as risen Messiah and Lord.

Paul's certainty is evidence for Paul's certainty.

His subsequent life is evidence that the experience was causally powerful.

Its external mechanism remains a separate question.

## 10. A Death Interpreted

A dead teacher creates a theological problem if followers thought God was vindicating him.

The problem generated answers.

His death became sacrifice.
Passover.
Atonement.
Victory.
Ransom.
Exaltation.
Fulfillment.
Cosmic turning point.
The defeat of powers.
The beginning of new creation.

These interpretations are not interchangeable, even when later doctrine combines them.

Scripture was reread around the death. Psalms, Isaiah, Zechariah, Passover imagery, Temple language, sacrifice, covenant, and royal texts became part of the interpretive field.

This is not proof that every reused scripture originally predicted Yeshua.

It is proof that early Christians searched Israel's scriptures for a grammar large enough to contain what had happened to them.

## 11. The Event Outside the Page

There is a limit here that writing cannot solve.

If resurrection was an act of God, historical method cannot climb outside the world and watch God perform it.

If resurrection was a visionary process, historical method cannot replay every brain and relationship that produced it.

If resurrection was a combination of grief, revelation, memory, social contagion, and later narrative, we still lack the complete causal chain.

If something else occurred, we are not owed a category for it.

Book VII will later ask what re-instantiation could mean in a simulated world.

That is not evidence here.

Book IV ends with a movement changed by the conviction that death had not won.

Whether that conviction was true in the largest possible sense remains open.

Historically, one thing is not open:

The execution intended to end the problem.

It did not.
