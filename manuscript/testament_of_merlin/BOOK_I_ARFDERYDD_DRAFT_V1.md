# Book I Draft V1 — Arfderydd

Status: AUTHORED CONSTRUCTION INSIDE HISTORICAL CONSTRAINT

## I

Before anyone called him Merlin, there was a field.

The field did not know the name Arfderydd.

It knew water under the turf and the weight of men.

It knew the old road pressed hard through the wet country, the low ridges where a shield wall could hold for a little while, the river crossings that mattered because horses tired and carts sank and no king, however beloved, could move an army through mud by wishing it otherwise.

The men gave names to everything because men feared what could not be named.

The field did not care.

Morning came grey.

Mist lay low over the ground. It caught in the thorn and folded itself around the legs of the horses. Somewhere beyond sight, men struck iron against iron to wake edges that would be blunt before noon.

He sat with his back against a cart wheel and listened.

Not to prophecy.

To ordinary things.

Leather stretching.

A horse coughing.

Someone retching quietly behind a wagon.

Two boys arguing over whose turn it was to fetch water.

A man praying badly because he had never expected to need the words.

These were the sounds by which the world announced that men intended to kill one another.

He knew some of their names.

That was the beginning of the trouble.

A stranger could become a shape beyond a spearpoint. A man whose mother he had met could not.

Gwenddolau's people moved through the camp with the purposeful irritability of men trying not to look frightened. Whatever later poets would make of their lord, that morning he required food, messengers, horses, men who understood the ground, and men who would stand when standing became irrational.

The survivor—because that is all we can safely call him here—had once believed battles were decided by courage.

He had been younger then.

Now he knew they were also decided by wet bowstrings, lame horses, forgotten grain, a cart axle splitting three miles from where it was needed, a messenger hearing one word where another had been spoken.

Kings received songs because no one wanted to sing about axles.

"Eat."

The man beside him held out a piece of oat bread.

He shook his head.

"You'll wish you had."

"I already wish I had yesterday."

The man laughed once.

That laugh remained.

Years later, when faces had begun to thin at the edges of memory, the laugh would still arrive complete.

He took the bread.

## II

The armies did not begin as armies.

They became armies as the mist lifted.

First there were men in knots and households, each gathered around the people they trusted. Then standards rose. Riders moved. Orders crossed the field. The separate groups took their places until the valley held two intentions opposed to one another.

Later sources would disagree about who stood against whom.

That was fitting.

No one standing there possessed the list that historians would eventually want.

He knew only that men were coming.

He knew that Gwenddolau had enemies.

He knew some enemies had enemies of their own.

He knew alliances made in winter had a way of becoming ambiguous when men could finally see the faces they would have to kill.

He knew the other side occupied more ground than he wanted them to.

Someone near him said the sons of Eliffer had come.

Someone else said there were more.

A third man swore Rhydderch was behind it all.

Years later, each version would find a manuscript.

That morning, none of them had footnotes.

The first arrows were disappointing.

No black cloud.

No sun blotted from the sky.

A scattering of thin lines climbed and disappeared and returned with gravity.

Men raised shields.

One screamed.

Then more.

The survivor had expected terror to feel like terror.

Instead his mind became precise.

A gap between two shields.

A horse turning sideways.

The angle of a spear.

The mud on a man's left boot.

The little decisions arrived faster than thought, and his body made them.

Move.

Stop.

Lower.

Strike.

Turn.

Again.

Again.

Again.

There was no battle now. There was only the next distance.

## III

Much later, people would say he went mad because his lord died.

That is a clean sentence.

Clean sentences are made after blood dries.

What happened on the field was not clean.

Men did not fall as they did in poems. Some folded. Some sat down. Some looked offended. One man spent a very long time trying to put something back inside himself with both hands.

The survivor stepped over him.

He would remember that.

Not because the man was important.

Because he stepped over him.

There are actions so small that no law notices them and so permanent that the person who performed them never leaves them.

By midday he had lost the ability to understand the scale of what he was seeing.

A hundred dead men and one dead man occupied the same amount of mind.

The world narrowed.

There was a raven standing on a shield.

There was blood in a hoofprint.

There was a yellow flower flattened into mud.

There was Gwenddolau somewhere ahead.

Or there had been.

A word moved backward through the line.

At first it was not a word. It was behavior.

Men turned their heads.

A standard dipped.

Someone shouted that the lord was down.

Someone else shouted that he was not.

A rider came through with blood covering one side of his face.

"Hold."

That was the order.

Hold.

The survivor looked at the faces around him and understood something before anyone said it plainly.

They did not believe they were holding for victory anymore.

They were holding because the alternative was admitting that the world in which the morning had begun no longer existed.

Then the line opened.

Not everywhere.

Not dramatically.

A few men went backward.

Others filled the space.

Then those men went backward too.

The survivor watched organization become motion and motion become flight.

Songs would later call men faithful.

Songs had that luxury.

## IV

He did not remember deciding to leave the field.

For years this troubled him.

He remembered staying.

He remembered fighting.

He remembered a man without a helmet swinging at him too slowly.

He remembered pushing someone away with the rim of his shield.

He remembered seeing the gold at his own throat when he looked down, absurdly bright beneath everything that had landed on him.

Then he remembered trees.

Nothing between.

People would eventually fill the missing distance for him.

They would say he fled.

Perhaps he did.

They would say madness took him.

Perhaps that word was as good as any.

But madness implies a border.

One moment sane.

One moment not.

The field offered no such courtesy.

The field entered him by degrees.

A scream he could still hear after the screaming stopped.

A face appearing where no face was.

The conviction that someone walked behind him whenever leaves shifted.

The certainty, for three nights, that if he slept the dead would discover they had been abandoned.

He moved north.

Or thought he did.

He avoided roads because roads carried men.

He avoided houses because houses contained questions.

At a stream he drank until he vomited and then drank again.

When darkness came he crawled beneath thorn and woke clawing at soil.

On the second morning he found blood on his hands.

He did not know whose.

That frightened him more than if he had known.

## V

There is a temptation here.

This is where a storyteller can give him the vision.

The heavens open.

A voice speaks his name.

The future arrives whole.

The man becomes the prophet.

That story exists.

But not here.

Not yet.

Here there was only a survivor listening so hard that ordinary sounds became unbearable.

Wind in birch.

Water over stone.

A distant dog.

Birds lifting suddenly from a ridge.

Each sound meant something because everything had meant death yesterday.

He began to notice patterns because not noticing them felt dangerous.

Birds rose before riders appeared.

Deer moved away from men long before men could be heard.

Smoke revealed settlements.

Silence revealed hunters.

Clouds revealed weather.

Tracks revealed direction.

A broken stem revealed passage.

The future, he discovered, often announced itself quietly.

Perhaps that was the first prophecy.

Not knowing tomorrow.

Seeing today before other people did.

## VI

By the time men came looking for survivors, he had learned not to answer voices.

Once, from the trees, he watched three riders pass.

They wore colors he recognized.

That should have comforted him.

Instead his heart struck so hard against his ribs that he crouched with both hands over his mouth until they were gone.

He understood then that he could not return merely because returning was permitted.

Something had changed its allegiance inside him.

The camp, the hall, the fire, the boast, the cup passed hand to hand—these belonged to the same species of world as the field.

The trees did not ask who had won.

For the first time since morning at Arfderydd, nothing required him to choose a side.

He slept beside an apple tree that night.

Much later a poem would remember an apple tree.

That does not mean it was this one.

But every legend eventually chooses a tree.

## VII

Years afterward, men would ask why he went into the forest.

They would prefer an answer.

Grief.

Guilt.

God.

Demons.

Prophecy.

Madness.

Cowardice.

Holiness.

All were better than uncertainty because each made the wilderness intelligible.

He had no answer for them.

He went because the next step was away from people.

Then he took another.

And another.

By the time the world decided he had vanished, he had simply continued.
