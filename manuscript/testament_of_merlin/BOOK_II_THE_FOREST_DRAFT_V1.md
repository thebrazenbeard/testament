# Book II Draft V1 — The Forest

Status: AUTHORED CONSTRUCTION INSIDE EARLY-TRADITION CONSTRAINT

## I

The forest taught him distance.

At first he measured everything from Arfderydd.

Three days from the field.

A week from the field.

The first frost since the field.

The first winter.

Then the field ceased to be a place behind him and became a direction inside him.

He stopped counting.

The country did not become kind.

That was something later people misunderstood about wilderness.

The forest did not heal him.

It did not know he was injured.

Rain wet him. Cold hurt him. Roots tripped him. Hunger reduced every philosophy to the problem of the next mouthful.

What the forest gave him was indifference.

A king could not command rain.

A priest could not condemn winter.

A dead lord could not demand that oak leaves fall upward.

Things happened because the conditions for them had arrived.

For a man whose human world had torn itself open over allegiance, that impersonality felt close to mercy.

## II

He learned the pigs before he learned himself.

Not domestic pigs at first.

Wild ones.

He knew where they had passed because the earth was opened by their snouts. He knew where they slept. He knew when to stay away.

Later he found a smaller animal wandering near a clearing, perhaps escaped from people, perhaps merely accustomed to them.

It watched him.

He watched it.

"You are very badly placed," he told it.

The pig continued eating.

He laughed.

The sound frightened both of them.

It was the first time he had heard himself laugh since the field.

That night the animal remained somewhere beyond the firelight.

The next morning it was still there.

He began speaking to it because conversation without expectation is easier than conversation with people.

He warned it about dogs.

He warned it about hunters.

He warned it about men who owned land.

The animal listened exactly as well as kings had.

Centuries later a poem would place a little pig before the wild prophet and preserve something that looked like tenderness inside a corpus mostly remembered for prediction.

Whether any sixth-century man ever spoke to such an animal is unknowable.

But the tradition remembered the prophet talking downward, not upward.

To a pig.

To a tree.

To living things beneath the notice of kings.

That was worth preserving.

## III

The apple tree came later.

Or perhaps earlier.

Memory did not keep sequence as well as scribes would.

There were several apple trees.

One grew at the edge of a place where the ground opened enough for sunlight.

Its branches had gone mostly wild. Small fruit hardened among the leaves.

He slept beneath it when he could.

In autumn he ate what fell.

When storms came he learned which side of the trunk stayed driest.

A tree becomes intimate through repetition.

He knew the split branch.

The place where bark curled away.

The hollow that held rain.

The roots exposed like knuckles.

He knew when birds arrived before he saw them because the branch moved differently beneath their weight.

One morning he found a cut in the bark.

Human.

Fresh.

He touched it and felt fear rise with ridiculous speed.

Not for himself.

For the tree.

Men marked trees before taking them.

He knew that.

The thought arrived whole: they will cut it down.

He spent the day watching the path.

No one came.

The mark remained.

He began talking to the tree too.

## IV

People later called this madness.

Perhaps it was.

But madness is often a name given by witnesses who prefer not to ask whether the altered person is responding coherently to an altered world.

He had learned that men could become dangerous in groups.

He had learned that ordinary loyalties could end in fields of opened bodies.

He had learned that the difference between safety and death might be a bird leaving a branch thirty breaths before a rider came into view.

So he watched everything.

He listened to everything.

And because the mind hates unused information, it began making relationships.

A red dawn after a certain wind.

Deer moving downhill before hard weather.

Ravens gathering where men had passed.

The way armed riders traveled differently from hunters.

The smell of rain.

The silence before snow.

The political world had patterns too.

A lord who demanded twice the grain would demand men next.

A messenger arriving without an escort meant haste.

A marriage meant a border had moved before any army did.

A feast could be a truce or the preparation for murder.

He had known these things before Arfderydd.

Afterward he could not stop knowing them.

People began asking what would happen.

That was the second beginning.

## V

The first man to call him prophet did not mean it as praise.

He had come upon the forest-dweller near dusk and nearly put a spear through him.

The survivor raised both hands.

The stranger stared.

Hair too long.

Clothes repaired beyond repair.

Feet wrapped in hide.

A face no hall would have welcomed.

"Who are you?"

The survivor considered the question.

He had once answered it with ancestry.

Then allegiance.

Then lord.

All three answers had become dangerous.

"No one."

The stranger made the sign of the cross.

That was almost funny.

"Are you a Christian?"

"Sometimes."

The stranger did not laugh.

"What are you doing here?"

"Watching you make too much noise."

The spear lowered slightly.

The survivor looked at the man's boots, the mud dried high along the calves, the sweat on the horse, the empty second saddle, the tear at the rider's sleeve.

"You lost someone."

The stranger's expression changed.

"Where?"

"South of here."

"How do you know?"

The survivor pointed at the horse.

"You rode north hard. There were two of you when you began."

The man stared long enough for inference to become miracle.

That transformation would happen many times.

## VI

He learned eventually that people did not want prediction.

They wanted permission.

Will my lord win?

Will my son return?

Will the Saxons come?

Will winter break?

Will this king die?

Will God punish them?

Will God punish us?

The honest answer was usually uncertainty.

No one crossed a forest to hear uncertainty.

So he learned another kind of speech.

"If he keeps men hungry, he will discover how little loyalty weighs."

"If your son follows that road in flood, pray less for victory than for bridges."

"If they build their houses on another man's fear, eventually they will live inside fear themselves."

People carried these sentences away.

By the time they returned, they were prophecies.

By the time scribes received them, they had dates.

By the time kings feared them, they had become ancient.

## VII

There were still nights when none of this mattered.

Nights when the field returned complete.

Not remembered.

Present.

The body does not distinguish history from recurrence when recurrence is vivid enough.

He woke with his cheek in wet leaves and someone's death in his mouth.

He would walk until sunrise.

Sometimes he saw light where no light should be.

Sometimes movement.

Once the entire sky seemed to split between branches.

He fell to his knees.

He never knew afterward whether he had fainted.

Later stories would become less uncertain.

They would give the light an origin.

They would put words inside it.

They would make angels or demons responsible.

A story dislikes an unexplained sky.

He kept only the terror.

## VIII

Winter narrowed him.

His beard whitened before he was old enough for it.

People saw age where there was exposure.

He learned which roots could be eaten and which merely looked as though they could.

He learned that cold made pride expensive.

Once he stole a cloak.

This never entered the prophecies.

He returned it three days later to the edge of the same farm, along with a rabbit.

This also did not enter them.

Legend has little patience for transactions that make a man ordinary.

## IX

He heard the name Lailoken years before he accepted that someone meant him.

Or perhaps that happened to another wild man.

The record does not permit certainty.

A boy shouted it across water.

"Lailoken!"

He looked behind himself.

The boy laughed and ran.

The name came again months later from a woman leaving bread on a stone.

He asked what it meant.

She shrugged.

"You."

That was how a name could begin.

Not birth.

Not prophecy.

Use.

The next winter another traveler called him something else.

Names gathered at the edge of the forest like offerings no one had asked him to carry.

## X

The name Myrddin had not reached him.

That matters.

Or, more precisely, our evidence does not allow us to know that it had.

The temptation is enormous to place it in his mouth now.

To let him say:

I am Myrddin.

To give the reader the satisfaction of recognition.

But recognition belongs to us.

Not necessarily to him.

So when a young man found him beneath the apple tree and asked, "What shall I call you?"

the survivor looked up through the branches.

Rain gathered at the tips.

One drop grew heavy enough to fall.

"Call me when the dogs are gone," he said.
