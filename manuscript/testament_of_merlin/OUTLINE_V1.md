# The Testament of Merlin — Outline V1

Status: EXPLORATORY AUTHORED CONSTRUCTION

## Prologue — The Name

A medieval copyist writes "Merlin went mad" beside the record of an old battle.

The line feels ancient.

It is not.

Open by making the reader watch history acquire a sentence that later generations will mistake for memory.

Evidence class:
- annalistic battle: HISTORICAL_ANCHOR
- "Merlin went mad" in later witness: LATER_TRADITION
- copyist interiority/dialogue: AUTHORED_CONSTRUCTION

## Book I — Arfderydd

Northern Britain, c. 573.

Build the political world around the battle without pretending that every later participant list is secure. Gwenddolau, the sons of Eliffer, Rhydderch, and the fractured Brittonic kingdoms form the historical horizon.

The central figure is not initially called Merlin.

He is a warrior, bard, retainer, observer, or composite human focal point whose precise social position remains deliberately unresolved.

The battle destroys not only people but a social world.

Narrative endpoint: he survives.

Historical rule:
Do not state as fact that a real Merlin fought there.

## Book II — The Forest

The survivor withdraws north into the wooded country remembered later as the Caledonian Forest.

This is where the literary work may take the greatest freedom, clearly marked downstream.

He experiences:
- grief;
- hypervigilance;
- altered time perception;
- intrusive sensory memory;
- isolation;
- pattern-finding;
- animal companionship;
- hunger and exposure;
- moments he understands as knowledge arriving from outside himself.

The text never has to decide whether his visions are:
- supernatural;
- trauma-generated;
- poetic construction;
- exceptional inference;
- or several things simultaneously.

The reader experiences the ambiguity from inside him.

## Book III — The Apple Tree and the Pig

The literary world moves toward the material later preserved in `Afallennau` and `Oianau`.

He talks to living things because human company has become intolerable or inaccessible.

Prophecy develops as a form of speech.

But later political prophecies intrude into the manuscript centuries afterward.

The book should therefore fracture typographically or narratively: the sixth-century voice is gradually invaded by later Welsh hopes and Norman-era politics.

The reader sees tradition editing the prophet in real time.

Critical constraint:
The oldest-looking forest speaker remains unnamed.

## Book IV — Lailoken

Shift viewpoint.

Other people encounter the wilderness figure.

Now he has a name—or perhaps a nickname—Lailoken/Laloecen.

Clerics and court figures interpret him through Christian categories:
madman, sinner, holy fool, prophet, cursed man.

Rhydderch's historically better-attested court can provide the political environment, while any direct relationship between Rhydderch and the wild man remains authored construction unless specifically source-bound.

The threefold-death tradition appears here not as certain biography but as a story people tell about him before or after his death.

Theme:
A person begins becoming a legend before he is dead.

## Book V — Myrddin

Move south and forward in time.

The prophetic authority called Myrddin is now Welsh.

Carmarthen/Caerfyrddin exerts gravitational pull on the name.

The book explores how geography can create biography: a town-name, a prophet-name, fragments of northern memory, and political prophecy combine.

Taliesin enters not as a proven historical meeting partner but as another legendary prophetic authority placed into dialogue with Myrddin.

The key question becomes:

**When two remembered voices are made to speak to one another centuries later, whose conversation is it?**

## Book VI — The Boy Beneath the Tower

Return to the older Ambrosius/Vortigern story.

A fatherless boy explains why Vortigern's construction fails and interprets the red and white dragons.

Crucially, he is not yet Merlin.

Tell this episode independently first.

Only after the reader knows Ambrosius as his own literary figure should the later fusion begin.

Theme:
Legends do not merely grow. They annex one another.

## Book VII — Geoffrey

England/Wales, twelfth century.

Geoffrey of Monmouth becomes a principal viewpoint.

He encounters histories, genealogies, prophecies, oral material, names, political needs, and incompatible versions.

He creates **Merlinus Ambrosius**.

His motives must remain authored reconstruction; do not state private intentions as historical fact.

The literary drama is the act of synthesis itself.

He sees that one prophetic figure can solve several narrative problems at once:
- Vortigern's tower;
- British prophecy;
- Uther;
- Arthur's conception;
- continuity between Britain's legendary ages.

Then, in the later `Vita Merlini`, the northern wild man comes flooding back into Merlin.

Geoffrey has made a person who cannot remain internally consistent because the source traditions were never one life.

## Book VIII — The Magician

Later romance inherits Geoffrey's creation.

Merlin acquires:
- expanded magical ability;
- shapeshifting;
- engineered royal conception;
- Arthurian mentorship;
- enchantresses;
- imprisonment or withdrawal;
- timelessness.

This section should accelerate through retellings, showing how each generation selects the Merlin it needs.

The possible sixth-century survivor is now almost inaccessible inside the cultural machine built from him.

## Book IX — The Testament

Strip the wizard back down.

Battle.
Forest.
Voice.
Names.
Copies.
Stories.

Do not reveal whether there "really was" one Merlin.

Instead expose the stronger conclusion:

A culture can preserve something true about catastrophe, altered people, political fear, wilderness, prophecy, and memory even when biography becomes unrecoverable.

The final question is not whether Merlin had magic.

It is whether the human being—if there was one—would recognize himself in what we made of him.

## Recurring structural device

Each major section may open with an evidence label:

**WHAT SURVIVES**
What the sources actually preserve.

**WHAT MAY HAVE HAPPENED**
Bounded reconstruction.

**WHAT WE ARE INVENTING**
Narrative construction.

These labels can later be moved to an apparatus if they interrupt the prose, but they should remain during drafting to prevent evidentiary drift.
